\begin{abstract}
Overdispersed count rates refers to phenomena in which the count variance exceeds the average count rate. Examples include looking for diamonds in carbon-rich sedimentary layers, counting photons in gamma-ray observation, or the spike count rate in populations of neurons. We propose a procedure for sequential Bayesian experimental design under a Poisson likelihood whose rate is modulated multiplicatively with an exposure function. The prior distribution over the rate parameter is a generalized inverse Gaussian prior, which is conjugate and yields an exact posterior predictive distributed as the Sichel law. Expected information gain is then calculated from two deterministic quadrature operations, providing a faster alternative to nested Monte Carlo sampling.
We show that the posterior concentrates if the accumulated exposure diverges, which is a persistence-of-excitation condition on exposure rather than on sample size. The proposed procedure is compared to state-of-the-art methods in three natural settings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Counting is fundamental to many fields of science and engineering. For example, in astronomy, photons are recorded over a period of time, and, in ecology, a camera trap counts the number of wolves passing by per week. In all these experiments, an experimenter has to choose how long the detector keeps counting, refered to as dwell time. Using the information obtained so far to optimize dwell time is called sequential optimal experimental design \citep{lindley1956measure,chaloner1995bayesian}. In phenomena with \emph{overdispersed} counts, the variance of the counts exceeds the average count. For example, in gamma ray observation, the detector will often pick up barely anything but occasionally it will detect millions of photons, which means its count variance is orders of magnitude larger than the average count. The overdispersed natura of these phenomena is a challenge for sequential experimental design.

The expected information gain is an expectation of a Kullback-Leibler divergence over a predictive distribution \citep{ryan2016review,huan2024optimal}. In a conjugate family such as Gamma-Poisson, the predictive is negative binomially distributed which lacks the expressiveness to capture tail probabilities accurately. Outside a conjugate family, the expected information gain must be estimated by nested Monte Carlo, which is computationally expensive and ill-suited for sequential design with short dwell times \citep{rainforth2018nesting,rainforth2024modern}. We opt to stay in a conjugate family, and will use the generalised inverse Gaussian distribution as a prior for the count rate. Its predictive is the Sichel distribution which has an additional parameter to describe tail probabilities and allows for deterministic quadrature procedures for calculating expected information gain \citep{sichel1975distribution,stein1987parameter}.

\paragraph{Related work.}
In active learning the same quantity is BALD \citep{houlsby2011bald}, with maximum entropy sampling \citep{sebastiani2000maximum} dropping the aleatoric term and EPIG \citep{smith2023prediction} the prediction-oriented alternative; it also appears as sensor management \citep{kreucher2005sensor,hero2008information,yu2009active}, source search \citep{vergassola2007infotaxis,hutchinson2018entrotaxis} and astronomical scheduling \citep{loredo2004bayesian}.
Across that work expected information gain is estimated rather than computed, and those estimators carry a bias no sample size removes \citep{rainforth2024modern}.
We give a count family in which the quantity they approximate is available directly, taking a mixing law from the classical conjugate analysis of exponential families \citep{diaconis1979conjugate,gutierrez1997exponential,chen2003conjugate} with one parameter more.

\section{Problem statement} \label{sec:problem}

Let $y \in \mathbb{N}$ be a set of counts and $\tau \in [0, \infty)$ be a dwell time. There are $K$ contexts, with context $k$ emitting events at an unknown rate $\lambda_k$. A design $u = (k,\tau)$ is a context and dwell time selection. Designs are chosen one at a time, until the time budget is spent. Let $\mathcal{D}_t = \{(y_i, u_i)\}_{i=1}^{t}$ be the designs selected and counts observed up to time $t$, and $\mathcal{V} = \{(y_j, u_j)\}_{j=1}^{n}$ be a held out test set of designs and counts.

There are three metrics of interest. First, data must be acquired such that the average surprisal on the test set is minimal. The surprisal is calculated as the negative log probability of test data under a posterior predictive distribution:
\begin{equation}
	S = \sum_{j=1}^{n} - \ln p(y_j \given u_j, \mathcal{D}_t) \, .
\end{equation}
Secondly, the model must identify as quickly as possible the context for which the count rate is maximal, $k^{*}$. That estimate is extracted from a posterior distribution over $\lambda_k$ that evolves over time, i.e., $\hat{k}_t = \arg \max_{k} p(\lambda_k \given \mathcal{D}_t)$. The goal is to minimize the regret up to time $T$,
\begin{equation}
	R = \sum_{t=1}^T | k^* - \hat{k}_t | \, .
\end{equation}
Lastly, we time models on the computational cost of inference, in terms of average number of seconds to select a new design.

\section{Model specification}
\label{sec:model}

Counts are modelled as Poisson distributed with rate parameter $\lambda$,
\begin{equation}
    p(y \given u, \lambda_k) = \mathcal{P}\big(y \given f(u) \lambda_k \big) \,,
    \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
and exposure function $f(u)$.

\begin{assumption}[Multiplicative exposure]
    \label{as:exposure}
    The action acts on the rate through a known scalar $f(u)$, which does not depend on the rates.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{as:exposure} carries the analytic results below: an action decides how much is measured and not which rate, and no action renders one rate observable through another.
The rates are independent under the prior and the likelihood factorises across contexts, so the rest of this section treats a single context and writes $\lambda$ for its rate.

The Fano factor is
\begin{equation}
    F(u) = 1 + f(u) \frac{\Var{p(\lambda)}{\lambda}}{\Ex{p(\lambda)}{\lambda}} \,.
    \label{eq:fano}
\end{equation}

Two properties of \eqref{eq:fano} matter.
First, the departure from equidispersion is a function of the action: a Poisson process has $F = 1$ everywhere, whereas here the second term grows linearly in the exposure, so how overdispersed the observations are is itself something the design decides.
Second, a negative binomial already has a free mean and variance, so overdispersion alone is no reason to leave the conjugate pair; what \eqref{eq:gig} adds is a third parameter which, with the predictive mean and variance held fixed, still moves mass between the centre and the tail, which is why the count-modelling literature reaches for the Sichel on data at once overdispersed, skewed and inflated at zero.

Write $\Gig$ for the generalised inverse Gaussian law,
%
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        p(\lambda) &= \Gig(\lambda \given \alpha, a, b) \\
        &= \frac{(b/a)^{\alpha/2}}{2 K_{\alpha}(\sqrt{ab})} \lambda^{\alpha - 1} \exp\big( -(a/\lambda + b \lambda)/2 \big) \,,
    \end{split}
    \label{eq:gig}
\end{equation}
%
with order $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, scale parameters $a, b > 0$, and $K_{\alpha}$ the modified Bessel function of the second kind.

Its three parameters separate once $(a, b)$ is traded for a scale $\eta = \sqrt{a/b}$ and a concentration $\omega = \sqrt{ab}$, because the mean of \eqref{eq:gig} is then
%
\begin{equation}
    \Ex{p(\lambda)}{\lambda} = \eta \frac{K_{\alpha + 1}(\omega)}{K_{\alpha}(\omega)} \,.
    \label{eq:gig_mean}
\end{equation}
%
Requiring a particular mean fixes $\eta$ in closed form and leaves $\alpha$ and $\omega$ free, so the brightness of a context and the shape of the uncertainty about it are set independently.

The gamma is a boundary of this family rather than a competitor: as $\omega \to 0$ with $\alpha > 0$, \eqref{eq:gig} tends to a gamma of shape $\alpha$ and rate $b/2$ and the marginal of \eqref{eq:likelihood} to a negative binomial, written $\NBin$.
The conjugate Poisson pair is therefore contained in the model, at one edge of a parameter it is otherwise free to move.

\section{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

A sequential design needs to know what a count tells it about the rate, and what to expect of the next count before committing to an action.
Conjugacy of the generalised inverse Gaussian with a Poisson likelihood makes both exact \citep{diaconis1979conjugate}, though neither survives evaluation in the arrangement in which it is most compactly written, which Remark~\ref{rem:conditioning} takes up.

\subsection{Updating parameters}
\label{sec:inference_update}

Under \eqref{eq:likelihood} and \eqref{eq:gig}, a count $y$ observed at action $u$ yields
\begin{equation}
    p(\lambda \given y, u) = \Gig\big(\lambda \given \alpha + y, \, a, \, b + 2 f(u) \big) \,. \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
Multiplying \eqref{eq:likelihood} by \eqref{eq:gig} and dropping every factor free of $\lambda$ leaves the kernel of \eqref{eq:gig} at the stated parameters.
The count enters only through the order and the action only through the second scale parameter, additively, so an exposure delivered in one measurement updates the belief exactly as the same exposure split across several.
The first scale parameter $a$ is never revised, because a Poisson likelihood carries information only through $\lambda^{y} e^{-f(u) \lambda}$ and neither factor has the $1/\lambda$ form that $a$ multiplies, so whatever weight the prior places on very small rates survives counting untouched. Repeating the update for $n$ time steps gives an update of $\nu + \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i$ and $b + \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(u_i)$, which is free of order.

A belief is therefore two numbers per context whatever the horizon, both sums, so the posterior does not depend on the order of measurement.
The second is chosen by the experimenter, which raises a question a fixed design need not answer.

%Write $F_t = \sum_{i \leq t} f(u_i)$, with the actions adapted, so $u_i$ may depend on everything observed before it.
The Fisher information up to time $t$ is:
\begin{equation}
	F_t = \mathbb{E} \big[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \lambda} \ln p(y \given u, \lambda_k) \given \lambda \big] = \sum{i < t} \frac{f(u_i)}{\lambda} \, .
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Posterior consistency]
    \label{prop:consistency}
    Fix a context with true rate $\lambda^{*} > 0$ and a prior \eqref{eq:gig} with $a, b > 0$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$.
    Since $F_t$ is non-decreasing it has a limit $F_{\infty} \in (0, \infty]$, and the two cases partition the sample space.
    On the event $\{F_{\infty} = \infty\}$,
    %
    \begin{equation}
        \Ex{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{(\lambda - \lambda^{*})^{2}} \longrightarrow 0
        \label{eq:consistency}
    \end{equation}
    %
    almost surely, so the posterior concentrates at $\lambda^{*}$.
    Its variance is $O(1/F_t)$, and more precisely
    %
    \begin{equation}
        F_t \, \Var{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{\lambda} \longrightarrow \lambda^{*}
        \label{eq:consistency_rate}
    \end{equation}
    %
    almost surely, which is the inverse Fisher information of a Poisson observed at total exposure $F_t$.
    On the event $\{F_{\infty} < \infty\}$ the posterior converges almost surely to a non-degenerate law and consistency fails.
\end{proposition}

The proof is in Appendix~\ref{appx:consistency}.
Its condition falls on accumulated exposure rather than on the number of measurements, so it restricts the design and not the sample size: exposures decaying fast enough to be summable stop learning at a finite spread however many follow.
This is persistence of excitation in the sense familiar from input design, the Fisher information accumulated being $F_t/\lambda^{*}$ by \eqref{eq:consistency_rate}.
The unrevised $a$ does not obstruct concentration, entering the proof only through a bracket of width proportional to $a$ and inversely to the accumulated count, and the constant in \eqref{eq:consistency_rate} is the best a Poisson likelihood allows.

\subsection{Posterior predictive distribution}
\label{sec:inference_predictive}

Because \eqref{eq:posterior} returns the family to itself, $(\alpha, a, b)$ denotes the current belief, prior and posterior alike, and the history is dropped from the notation.

\begin{proposition}[Posterior predictive]
    \label{prop:predictive}
    Under the belief $\Gig(\lambda \given \alpha, a, b)$, the count returned by action $u$ has mass function
    %
    \begin{equation}
        p(y \given u, \mathcal{D}) = \frac{(f(u) \eta_u)^{y}}{y!} \Big( \frac{\eta_u}{\eta} \Big)^{\alpha} \frac{K_{\alpha + y}(\omega_u)}{K_{\alpha}(\omega)} \,,
        \label{eq:sichel}
    \end{equation}
    %
    where $\eta_u = \sqrt{a / (b + 2 f(u))}$ and $\omega_u = \sqrt{a (b + 2 f(u))}$ are the scale and concentration of the updated belief in the coordinates of \eqref{eq:gig_mean}.
\end{proposition}

Equation~\eqref{eq:sichel} is the Sichel law $\Sic$ \citep{sichel1975distribution,stein1987parameter} and follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:update} without further integration, the marginal being the ratio of the two normalising constants times the rate-free factor of the Poisson mass.
Every dependence on the action is carried by $f(u)$ through $(\eta_u, \omega_u)$, so a grid of candidates is scored against a belief computed once; the support is unbounded, so a functional that is not a moment, the entropy in particular, is a truncated sum over it.

\begin{remark}[An exact form is not an evaluation recipe]
    \label{rem:conditioning}
    Equation~\eqref{eq:sichel} must not be evaluated as written.
    $K_{\alpha + y}$ overflows double precision at moderate order, and by Corollary~\ref{cor:sufficient} the order a belief carries is $\alpha + \sum_i y_i$, which grows without bound; in Section~\ref{sec:experiments_verification} a direct call overflows after four measurements.
    Every quantity is therefore accumulated in logarithms, and the run of orders a support sum needs comes by one of two routes.
    The recurrence $K_{\nu + 1}(z) = K_{\nu - 1}(z) + (2 \nu / z) K_{\nu}(z)$ adds two positive quantities, so nothing cancels, but its rounding compounds, reaching $3 \times 10^{-7}$ after $2 \times 10^{5}$ orders where the uniform asymptotic expansion is still exact.
    Short runs take the recurrence and long runs the expansion, and which is needed is set by the weight of the predictive tail.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Information gain per action}
\label{sec:inference_acquisition}

A candidate design is scored by the mutual information between $\lambda$ and $y$ under the current belief \citep{lindley1956measure,houlsby2011bald},
%
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        G(u) &= \Ex{p(y \given u, \mathcal{D})}{ D_{\text{KL}}\big[ p(\lambda \given y, u, \mathcal{D}) \,\|\, p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}) \big] } \\
        &= H\big[ p(y \given u, \mathcal{D}) \big] - A(u) \,,
    \end{split}
    \label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}
%
the second line being the usual rearrangement into a predictive entropy less an expected conditional one,
%
\begin{equation}
    A(u) = \Ex{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D})}{ H\big[ \mathcal{P}\big(y \given f(u) \lambda \big) \big] } \,.
    \label{eq:aleatoric}
\end{equation}

Under \eqref{eq:likelihood} and \eqref{eq:gig} both terms are integrals against known densities and neither needs a sample: the first is a sum of \eqref{eq:sichel} over the support of the predictive, the second a one-dimensional integral of the Poisson entropy against the belief.
Neither is closed form, the support being unbounded and the Poisson entropy having no elementary expression, but both are \emph{deterministic}, so \eqref{eq:eig} carries no Monte-Carlo error, no sample size to tune and no nesting bias, which is what an estimator outside a conjugate family must accept \citep{rainforth2018nesting,rainforth2024modern}.
Both are cheap because Proposition~\ref{prop:predictive} puts every dependence on the action in $f(u)$ and \eqref{eq:aleatoric} integrates over one positive variable.
The support sum is sized from \eqref{eq:predictive_mean} and \eqref{eq:predictive_variance} and widened until the tail mass outside meets a tolerance of $10^{-9}$, a normal-tail estimate not being conservative here; the second term is a $64$-node Gauss-Legendre rule in $\log \lambda$.
Both accumulate in logarithms, for the reason Remark~\ref{rem:conditioning} gives.

The decomposition in \eqref{eq:eig} separates the criteria compared in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}: EPIG \citep{smith2023prediction}, local D-optimality \citep{chaloner1995bayesian}, Neyman allocation \citep{neyman1992two}, uncertainty sampling in a total and an epistemic form \citep{griffiths2019heteroscedastic}, Thompson sampling, maximum entropy sampling \citep{sebastiani2000maximum}, and the belief-free systematic and random designs.
Maximum entropy sampling is \eqref{eq:eig} with $A(u)$ dropped, and a Poisson entropy grows with its mean, roughly as $\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e f(u) \lambda)$, so dropping $A(u)$ rewards an action for the noise it collects instead of the uncertainty it removes.

\begin{remark}[What a two-moment surrogate can and cannot see]
    \label{rem:dopt}
    Local D-optimality scores an action by $\log(1 + f(u) \Var{}{\lambda} / \Ex{}{\lambda})$, the log of the Fano factor \eqref{eq:fano}, so it reads the belief through one scalar.
    Carrying that approximation into both terms of \eqref{eq:eig} gives
    %
    \begin{equation}
        G(u) = \tfrac{1}{2} \log\big( 1 + f(u) \Var{}{\lambda} / \Ex{}{\lambda} \big) + c + o(1)
        \label{eq:dopt_limit}
    \end{equation}
    %
    as $f(u) \Ex{}{\lambda} \to \infty$, with $c = \tfrac{1}{2}( \log \Ex{}{\lambda} - \Ex{}{\log \lambda} ) - \Delta$ and $\Delta$ the entropy deficit of the predictive against a Gaussian of its variance.
    The first term is the D-optimality score exactly and $c$ depends on the belief but not the action, so the two are an affine transform of one another along the exposure axis: contexts whose priors share a shape share $c$ and are ranked identically by both, whatever their means and variances.
    Numerically $\Delta$ varies little across shapes, so $c$ moves almost entirely through the log-mean-minus-mean-log gap, a dispersion on the log scale the Fano factor cannot see.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Policy}
\label{sec:inference_policy}

The criterion \eqref{eq:eig} ranks actions but does not say what to do, because actions differ in what they cost: a budget spent in gravel or telescope time is not one counted in measurements, and criteria compared at equal numbers of measurements would favour whichever asks for the largest sample.
We therefore score an action by the information expected per unit of budget consumed, and act greedily,
%
\begin{equation}
    u_{t + 1} \in \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \; \frac{G(u)}{c(u)} \,,
    \label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
%
where $c(u) > 0$ is what the action costs.
We evaluate \eqref{eq:policy} over the grid of contexts and exposures, take the winner, update that context's belief by Proposition~\ref{prop:update} and repeat; the same normalisation is applied to every criterion in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, ours included.
Because the belief factorises an observation leaves the other $K - 1$ contexts untouched, so a step costs one update and $|\mathcal{V}|$ scores against the $K |\mathcal{V}|$ a naive implementation would pay.
The rule is greedy over one step, which nothing above requires; we take it because every criterion in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} is greedy in the same way.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Verification on GIG-Poisson data}
\label{sec:experiments_verification}

With rates drawn from \eqref{eq:gig} and counts from \eqref{eq:likelihood}, correct specification is arranged and any discrepancy is the implementation's.
Against $4 \times 10^{5}$ draws, \eqref{eq:sichel} carries a support mass of one to $4 \times 10^{-13}$, the update \eqref{eq:posterior} agrees with numerical Bayes to a total variation of $8 \times 10^{-13}$, and the log-ratio to the negative binomial falls as $\omega^{2}$.
Remark~\ref{rem:conditioning} is not hypothetical: a direct call to $K_{\nu}$ overflows at the fourth measurement, the order having reached $465.5$, while in log space the Bessel quantities stay accurate to $1.3 \times 10^{-13}$ against $40$-digit references.

Over four regimes, prediction error falls as $n^{-1}$ and rate error as $n^{-1/2}$, and rescaling the posterior variance by $F_n / \Ex{}{\lambda^{*}}$ collapses all four onto the constant \eqref{eq:consistency_rate} predicts to within $2$ per cent, despite prior variance-to-mean ratios spanning a factor of $50$; a misspecified gamma-Poisson arm converges at the same rate, paying a constant at the start and within two per cent by the thirty-second observation (Figure~\ref{fig:prediction}, Table~\ref{tab:prediction_slopes}).

This is the setting the Sichel law was introduced for: it was the failure of the standard discrete laws on stone counts in clustered diamond deposits that motivated the generalised inverse Gaussian \citep{sichel1975distribution,stein1987parameter}, to value a deposit once samples are in hand rather than to decide which to take.
A property has $K$ blocks of unknown stone density $\lambda_k$, and an action $(k, v)$ names a block and a volume of gravel from $\{1, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40\}$ cubic metres, with exposure $f(u) = v r_k$ through the plant's known recovery factor, which is Assumption~\ref{as:exposure} exactly.
The budget is gravel rather than samples, so every criterion is scored per cubic metre.
Each block's stone field is realised once as a Poisson process in processed volume, so two policies that process the same gravel recover the same stones.
We report a held-out predictive score, the grade error, and the grade forgone by committing to the four best-ranked blocks.

Table~\ref{tab:bulk} reports both comparisons at twelve blocks and $240$ cubic metres, and against every criterion that ignores the aleatoric term the result is decisive.
Maximum entropy sampling is the worst agent in the study, behind random, touching $2.8$ of $12$ blocks and putting $239$ of $240$ cubic metres into the richest block of a representative property: scoring a sample by how unpredictable its outcome is sends the budget where it teaches least per unit of gravel, which is $A(u)$ going unsubtracted.
Thompson sampling and both variance criteria fail the same way.

Against the criteria that use the same posterior as carefully the accuracy is a tie and what separates them is cost.
EPIG \citep{smith2023prediction} and D-optimality are indistinguishable from ours, and none separates on the coarse ranking: with one parameter per context, information about the rate and about the next count rank candidates almost identically.
Holding the acquisition fixed instead, the third parameter is worth $0.012$ nats against the gamma boundary of its own family and $0.006$ against a lognormal, both measured where our model is correct by construction, so they report what misspecification costs rather than what the parameter is worth.
Appendix~\ref{appx:grid} shows the two effects do not interact.

Remark~\ref{rem:dopt} says the two criteria must agree wherever contexts share a prior shape, which is how both studies above are arranged; this section varies it.

Twelve contexts fall in two classes of six, sharing a prior mean and differing in the order of the mixing law, $-2$ against $-0.5$.
The heavy class is given the \emph{larger} index of dispersion, $1.30$ against $1.00$, so local D-optimality strictly prefers it while \eqref{eq:eig} strictly prefers the mild class: confident and opposite preferences, neither ever breaking a tie, which a gate verifies before the sweep runs.
That direction is the interesting one, because a large index of dispersion under a heavy-tailed mixing law is mostly unresolvable, coming from a small probability of a very large rate that no exposure recovers, and D-optimality reads that variance as something to be learned.
Rates are drawn from each class's own law and every criterion carries the same belief, so the acquisition is all that varies.

Figure~\ref{fig:shape_scarcity} sweeps the budget at a low and a high count level and Table~\ref{tab:shape_scarcity} reports one point of it.
While the budget is scarce our criterion is ahead on the rate estimate, by $0.041$ dex at $-13.3$ standard errors at the scarcest point and $0.019$ at $-5.7$ with $0.67$ units of exposure per context, D-optimality putting the whole budget into the heavy class and ours none of it.
Lowering the counts roughly quadruples the gain at fixed budget, from $-3.2$ standard errors to $-13.3$, which is where the approximation behind \eqref{eq:dopt_limit} is weakest.

Three things qualify this.
The separation is on the rate estimate, not the held-out predictive, where D-optimality stays within two standard errors of us everywhere: \eqref{eq:eig} scores information about the rate, and the rate is what it moves.
EPIG matches us throughout, so what pays is the exact predictive rather than this functional, and both need the conjugacy of Section~\ref{sec:inference}.
And past about $1.3$ units per context the sign turns, D-optimality leading by $4.4$ standard errors at $2.67$: our greedy rule reinvests where the score is still high and has touched $74$ per cent of contexts there against D-optimality's hundred, so the concentration that wins under scarcity becomes under-coverage once the budget could reach everything, which is what a policy not ensuring the condition of Proposition~\ref{prop:consistency} costs.

This section asks three measured fields at once and lets them disagree: the photon fluxes of Section~\ref{sec:experiments_photon}, the count rates of $1107$ five-hundred-metre cells around Fukushima Daiichi recorded by the Safecast project \citep{brown2016safecast}, and, as a deliberate outsider, 
Each law is fitted by maximum likelihood with the location at zero and scored by AIC, so the third parameter pays for itself; no acquisition, budget or policy enters.

On the two physical fields the third parameter is decisive, by $1773$ and $568$ points of AIC against the gamma; on the linguistic field it is worth $7$ points on $22460$ observations, or $0.0003$ each.
The fitted order says why.
A gamma is the boundary of the family at $\omega \to 0$ with positive order, so a field fitted at $-1.43$ or $-2.07$ is one no gamma can reach however its two parameters are set, while one fitted at $+1.73$ merely sits inside the gamma's range, which is necessary for the gamma to suffice and not sufficient.

Three features of the data qualify this.
The Fermi fluxes come from a flux-limited catalogue, so the field is the detected population rather than the intrinsic one.
A Safecast cell's rate is a mean over vehicle passes spread across years of decay and neighbouring cells are not independent, so the effective sample size is below $1107$.
The Newsgroups field excludes terms absent from every group, removing the rare bursty terms where a heavy tail would show most, which works against the null result rather than for it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Assumption~\ref{as:exposure} keeps the family closed under observation but excludes an additive term: a real gamma-ray observation counts source photons together with a known diffuse background, so the rate is $f(u)\lambda + b$, the mixture is no longer conjugate and the predictive no longer Sichel.
That is the most concrete boundary of the framework we have found.

EPIG \citep{smith2023prediction} and local D-optimality \citep{chaloner1995bayesian} matched ours on accuracy in both allocation studies, at $0.52$, $8.9$ and $75$ milliseconds per decision.
Remark~\ref{rem:dopt} says why the second ties, and it is not a fact about those problems: D-optimality is the leading term of our own criterion, so contexts whose priors share a shape are ranked identically by both, and both studies are arranged that way.
Section~\ref{sec:experiments_shape} separates them where the theory says it should, though narrowly, and EPIG separates from us nowhere, which places the value in the exact predictive rather than in the choice of functional.
Conjugacy buys an exact predictive, an acquisition free of Monte-Carlo error and a decision no two-moment surrogate reaches in one identified regime; it does not buy the cheapest rule.

Against every criterion that ignores the aleatoric term the margin is wide: maximum entropy sampling \citep{sebastiani2000maximum} was the worst policy in both studies, behind uniform random sampling, and the failure reproduces under every mixing law we tried, so it belongs to the criterion and not to our family.

Once the budget is large enough that every context ends up well measured, no rule distinguishes itself from visiting each in turn, and the transition is sharp, so a study reporting one budget reports one point on that curve.
Past it ours is slightly behind D-optimality: Proposition~\ref{prop:consistency} needs accumulated exposure to diverge at every context and Section~\ref{sec:inference_policy} does not ensure it, the one place here where the theory and the policy come apart.
A coverage floor would close it and we have not tested one.

The two allocation studies are simulators whose structure is taken from their domains rather than field trials; only the rate fields of Section~\ref{sec:experiments_fields} are measured.
Contexts are independent, so a spatial problem in which neighbouring cells inform each other would break the factorisation.
The rate of a context is fixed and unknown, so a process in which it is redrawn at every observation is a different model.
\claude{That last point is unresolved and load-bearing: it decides what the right baselines are. See the memory note \texttt{overdispersion-has-two-readings}.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Counting experiments with a controllable exposure admit a conjugate model richer than the gamma-Poisson pair: mixing a Poisson rate over a generalised inverse Gaussian keeps the posterior in the family and makes the predictive exact, so expected information gain becomes two deterministic quadratures rather than a nested estimate, and the posterior concentrates if the design lets accumulated exposure diverge.
The resulting rule beats every criterion that ignores the aleatoric term once the budget cannot cover the contexts, and is the leading-order equal of the closest two-moment surrogate except under differing prior shape, a scarce budget and low counts.
Whether the third parameter earns its cost is a question about the field, answered from the rates beforehand.

\section{Proof of Posterior Consistency}
\label{appx:consistency}

Throughout, one context is fixed and its rate is written $\lambda$, with true value $\lambda^{*} > 0$.
Let $\mathcal{F}_{t}$ be the filtration generated by $(u_1, y_1, \dots, u_t, y_t)$ and let the policy be adapted, so that $u_{i}$ is $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$-measurable and may depend on everything observed before step $i$.
Write $f_i = f(u_i)$, and
%
\begin{equation*}
    T_t = \sum_{i \leq t} y_i \,, \qquad F_t = \sum_{i \leq t} f_i \,, \qquad \nu_t = \alpha + T_t \,, \qquad b_t = b + 2 F_t \,,
\end{equation*}
%
so that by Corollary~\ref{cor:sufficient} the posterior is $\Gig(\lambda \given \nu_t, a, b_t)$, with scale $\eta_t = \sqrt{a/b_t}$ and concentration $\omega_t = \sqrt{a b_t}$.
Two identities are used repeatedly, both immediate: $\eta_t / \omega_t = 1/b_t$ and $\eta_t \omega_t = a$.
The moments of the generalised inverse Gaussian are
%
\begin{equation}
    \Ex{}{\lambda^{n}} = \eta_t^{n} \frac{K_{\nu_t + n}(\omega_t)}{K_{\nu_t}(\omega_t)} \,,
    \label{eq:gig_moments_appx}
\end{equation}
%
so everything below is a statement about the ratio $R_{\nu}(z) = K_{\nu + 1}(z) / K_{\nu}(z)$.

\subsection{A Bracket on the Bessel Ratio}

The proof needs no asymptotic expansion of $K$.
An exact two-sided bound follows from the recurrence alone, and it is the only property of the Bessel functions used.

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:bessel_bracket}
    For every $z > 0$ and every real $\nu$,
    %
    \begin{equation}
        R_{\nu}(z) > \frac{2\nu}{z} \,,
        \label{eq:bracket_lower}
    \end{equation}
    %
    and if in addition $\nu > 1$,
    %
    \begin{equation}
        R_{\nu}(z) < \frac{2\nu}{z} + \frac{z}{2(\nu - 1)} \,.
        \label{eq:bracket_upper}
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    The modified Bessel function of the second kind is strictly positive on $(0, \infty)$ for every real order, and satisfies $K_{\nu + 1}(z) = K_{\nu - 1}(z) + (2 \nu / z) K_{\nu}(z)$.
    Dividing by $K_{\nu}(z)$ gives the exact recursion
    %
    \begin{equation}
        R_{\nu}(z) = \frac{2\nu}{z} + \frac{1}{R_{\nu - 1}(z)} \,,
        \label{eq:ratio_recursion}
    \end{equation}
    %
    valid for every real $\nu$.
    Positivity of $K$ makes $R_{\nu - 1}(z) > 0$, so the second term of \eqref{eq:ratio_recursion} is strictly positive and \eqref{eq:bracket_lower} follows.
    Applying \eqref{eq:bracket_lower} at order $\nu - 1$, which is a positive lower bound exactly when $\nu > 1$, gives $R_{\nu - 1}(z) > 2(\nu - 1)/z$ and hence $1/R_{\nu - 1}(z) < z / 2(\nu - 1)$.
    Substituting into \eqref{eq:ratio_recursion} gives \eqref{eq:bracket_upper}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{A Law of Large Numbers for the Accumulated Count}

\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:lln}
    On the event $\{F_t \to \infty\}$, $T_t / F_t \to \lambda^{*}$ almost surely, and $\nu_t \to \infty$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    Under $\lambda^{*}$ the conditional law of $y_i$ given $\mathcal{F}_{i - 1}$ is Poisson with mean $f_i \lambda^{*}$, since $f_i$ is $\mathcal{F}_{i-1}$-measurable.
    Hence $M_t = T_t - \lambda^{*} F_t$ is a square-integrable martingale with predictable quadratic variation
    %
    \begin{equation*}
        \langle M \rangle_t = \sum_{i \leq t} \Var{}{y_i \given \mathcal{F}_{i-1}} = \lambda^{*} F_t \,.
    \end{equation*}
    %
    On $\{F_t \to \infty\}$ we have $\langle M \rangle_{\infty} = \infty$, so the strong law for square-integrable martingales gives $M_t / \langle M \rangle_t \to 0$ almost surely, that is $T_t / F_t \to \lambda^{*}$.
    Since $\lambda^{*} > 0$ and $F_t \to \infty$, also $T_t \to \infty$ and therefore $\nu_t = \alpha + T_t \to \infty$.
\end{proof}

The adaptivity of the policy is handled entirely by this lemma, and it is the only place it is needed.
Nothing later distinguishes a sequential design from one fixed in advance.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:consistency}}

\begin{proof}
    Work first on $\{F_{\infty} = \infty\}$, intersected with the almost sure event of Lemma~\ref{lem:lln}, on which $\nu_t \to \infty$ and so $\nu_t > 1$ for all large $t$.

    \paragraph{The posterior mean.}
    Apply \eqref{eq:gig_moments_appx} at $n = 1$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:bessel_bracket} at $\nu = \nu_t$, $z = \omega_t$.
    Using $\eta_t / \omega_t = 1/b_t$ and $\eta_t \omega_t = a$ to simplify each side,
    %
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{2 \nu_t}{b_t} \; < \; \Ex{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{\lambda} \; < \; \frac{2 \nu_t}{b_t} + \frac{a}{2 (\nu_t - 1)} \,.
        \label{eq:mean_bracket}
    \end{equation}
    %
    The width of the bracket is $a / 2(\nu_t - 1) \to 0$, and the leading term satisfies
    %
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{2 \nu_t}{b_t} = \frac{2 F_t}{b + 2 F_t} \cdot \frac{\alpha + T_t}{F_t} \longrightarrow 1 \cdot \lambda^{*}
        \label{eq:mean_limit}
    \end{equation}
    %
    by Lemma~\ref{lem:lln}.
    Hence the posterior mean converges to $\lambda^{*}$ almost surely.
    Note that the first scale parameter $a$ appears nowhere except in the width of \eqref{eq:mean_bracket}, which is the formal content of the observation that $a$ is never revised and never needs to be.

    \paragraph{The posterior variance.}
    By \eqref{eq:gig_moments_appx}, $\Var{}{\lambda} = \eta_t^{2} R_{\nu_t}(R_{\nu_t + 1} - R_{\nu_t})$, with the argument $\omega_t$ suppressed.
    Applying \eqref{eq:ratio_recursion} at orders $\nu_t + 1$ and $\nu_t$ and discarding the negative term $-1/R_{\nu_t - 1}$,
    %
    \begin{equation*}
        R_{\nu_t + 1} - R_{\nu_t} = \frac{2}{\omega_t} + \frac{1}{R_{\nu_t}} - \frac{1}{R_{\nu_t - 1}} < \frac{2}{\omega_t} + \frac{\omega_t}{2 \nu_t} \,,
    \end{equation*}
    %
    where \eqref{eq:bracket_lower} bounds $1 / R_{\nu_t}$.
    Combining this with \eqref{eq:bracket_upper} for $R_{\nu_t}$ and expanding the product gives
    %
    \begin{equation}
        \Var{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{\lambda} \; < \; \frac{4 \nu_t}{b_t^{2}} + \frac{a}{b_t} \Big( 1 + \frac{1}{\nu_t - 1} \Big) + \frac{a^{2}}{4 \nu_t (\nu_t - 1)} \,.
        \label{eq:variance_bound}
    \end{equation}
    %
    By Lemma~\ref{lem:lln}, $\nu_t / F_t \to \lambda^{*}$ and $b_t / F_t \to 2$, so the first term of \eqref{eq:variance_bound} is asymptotic to $\lambda^{*} / F_t$, the second is of order $1/F_t$ and the third of order $1/F_t^{2}$.
    The posterior variance is therefore $O(1/F_t)$ and vanishes.

    \paragraph{Concentration.}
    The posterior mean-squared error decomposes as
    %
    \begin{equation*}
        \Ex{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{(\lambda - \lambda^{*})^{2}} = \Var{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{\lambda} + \Big( \Ex{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{\lambda} - \lambda^{*} \Big)^{2} \,,
    \end{equation*}
    %
    and both terms vanish almost surely by the two paragraphs above, which is \eqref{eq:consistency}.
    Concentration follows by Chebyshev's inequality, since for any $\epsilon > 0$,
    %
    \begin{equation*}
        p\big( |\lambda - \lambda^{*}| > \epsilon \given \mathcal{D}_t \big) \leq \epsilon^{-2} \Ex{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{(\lambda - \lambda^{*})^{2}} \longrightarrow 0 \,.
    \end{equation*}
    %
    The sharper statement \eqref{eq:consistency_rate} needs a finer bound on the same ratio and is proved in Appendix~\ref{appx:consistency_constant}.

    \paragraph{The converse.}
    Work now on $\{F_{\infty} < \infty\}$.
    Then $\sum_{i} \Pr(y_i > 0 \given \mathcal{F}_{i-1}) = \sum_{i} (1 - e^{-f_i \lambda^{*}}) \leq \lambda^{*} F_{\infty} < \infty$, so by the conditional Borel--Cantelli lemma only finitely many observations are nonzero almost surely.
    Hence $T_t \to T_{\infty} < \infty$, and the posterior converges to $\Gig(\lambda \given \alpha + T_{\infty}, a, b + 2 F_{\infty})$, which has strictly positive variance because $b + 2F_\infty < \infty$.
    The posterior does not concentrate, at any $\lambda^{*}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The Constant in the Rate}
\label{appx:consistency_constant}

Bound \eqref{eq:variance_bound} gives the order $1/F_t$ but not the sharp constant, because its second term contributes at the same order as the first.
The looseness is an artefact of bracketing $R_{\nu_t}$ and $R_{\nu_t + 1} - R_{\nu_t}$ separately, when the variance is a difference of two nearly equal quantities.
Substituting \eqref{eq:ratio_recursion} into itself once more sharpens \eqref{eq:bracket_upper} to
%
\begin{equation}
    R_{\nu}(z) = \frac{2\nu}{z} + \frac{z}{2(\nu - 1)} + O(z^{3}/\nu^{3}) \,,
    \label{eq:ratio_expansion}
\end{equation}
%
uniformly as $\nu / z \to \infty$, which holds here because $\nu_t / \omega_t$ grows like $\lambda^{*} \sqrt{F_t / 2a}$.
The correction term must be kept at $\nu - 1$ rather than $\nu$, since the two differ by $O(z/\nu^{2})$ and that is larger than $O(z^{3}/\nu^{3})$ in this regime.
Differencing \eqref{eq:ratio_expansion} at consecutive orders cancels the correction to leading order, because $z/2\nu - z/2(\nu - 1) = -z / 2\nu(\nu - 1)$, and leaves $R_{\nu_t + 1} - R_{\nu_t} = 2/\omega_t + O(\omega_t / \nu_t^{2})$.
Hence
%
\begin{equation}
    F_t \Var{p(\lambda \given \mathcal{D}_t)}{\lambda} = \frac{4 \nu_t F_t}{b_t^{2}} \big( 1 + o(1) \big) \longrightarrow \lambda^{*} \,,
    \label{eq:variance_rate}
\end{equation}
%
which is the inverse Fisher information of a Poisson observed at total exposure $F_t$ and proves \eqref{eq:consistency_rate}.
Simulation agrees.
Across true rates $\lambda^{*} \in \{0.2, 3, 50\}$, orders $\alpha \in \{-3, -0.5, 2\}$ and a prior mean deliberately set to $1$ in every case, the ratio $F_t \Var{}{\lambda} / \lambda^{*}$ reaches $1.000$ to $1.007$ by $F_t = 10^{5}$.
